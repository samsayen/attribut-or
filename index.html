Cyber threat attribution, explained

Who did it, and why every vendor calls them something else

Attribution is a chain of inferences from artefacts to activity clusters to organisations to people. Each vendor builds that chain from its own telemetry, so each draws the cluster boundary in a slightly different place and gives it its own name. This page walks through the models, the names, the evidence, and who has the access to see it.

One operator set, many names
Part 1

The models analysts reason with

No single model produces an attribution. Analysts use these together: one to structure an intrusion, one to describe behaviour, one to judge how durable an indicator is, and one to keep their own bias in check.

Part 2

The naming problem

A name is a label on one vendor's cluster of observed activity. Two names are "the same actor" only to the extent the two clusters overlap. Sometimes that is near total (APT28 and Fancy Bear). Sometimes one vendor's group is a slice of another's, or two vendors split what a third lumps together.

In June 2025 Microsoft and CrowdStrike published a joint alias mapping, with Google/Mandiant and Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 contributing. It is a translation table. Each vendor still keeps its own names and its own cluster boundaries.

Part 4

Who can see what

The strength of an attribution depends on vantage point. A victim sees one intrusion. A researcher may see the attacker's server. A hyperscaler sees the attacker's accounts. A government may be sitting on the attacker's own keyboard.

Part 5 · Deep dive

North Korea and the open-source supply chain

DPRK operators are tasked with raising hard currency as well as collecting intelligence. Developers hold wallet keys, cloud credentials, signing certificates and CI tokens, and they install code from public registries every day. That makes the open-source supply chain the shortest path from a fake job offer to a nine-figure theft.

Signals that recur across DPRK attributions

  • Egress habits. Astrill VPN exits, proxies in the Russian Far East, and occasional direct connections from North Korea's small public netblock when a VPN drops.
  • Malware lineage. New families reuse loaders, string encoding, C2 protocol structure and build paths from older ones (AppleJeus, BeaverTail, WAVESHAPER).
  • Money trail. Stolen assets move through wallets and laundering routes that blockchain analysts have already tied to earlier DPRK heists.
  • Persona reuse. The same recruiter profiles, email addresses, GitHub accounts and front companies show up in separate campaigns, and overlap with the remote IT worker scheme.
  • Target fit. Crypto exchanges, wallet developers, DeFi teams and the developers who work for them, which matches the regime's revenue mission more than any criminal group's.
  • Platform records. Registries, code hosts and cloud providers hold sign-up and login data for the attacker accounts and share it with investigators.
Part 6

eCrime: brands, clusters and people

State groups are organisations with a chain of command, so a name can stay stable for a decade. English-speaking eCrime is a loose social scene. The same people work under several brands, brands are shared or copied, and a vendor's cluster number often describes a technique more than a fixed crew. Keep three layers apart: the ecosystem, the tracked cluster, and the brand used in the extortion note.

Sources and limits

Alias mappings are approximations drawn from public vendor reporting as of November 2026: Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant, Microsoft Threat Intelligence, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, Secureworks (Sophos), ESET, Kaspersky, Trend Micro, Proofpoint, Recorded Future, Socket, ReversingLabs, RH-ISAC, plus US DOJ indictments, FBI and CISA advisories, and UK NCSC statements. Where a cell says "no public equivalent", that vendor may track the activity privately or under a name not mapped publicly.

Specific 2026 items worth checking against the primary reports before you cite them: the axios npm compromise (Google's write-up), the BlackFile cluster (RH-ISAC and Unit 42), and the StegaBin npm wave (Socket). The workbench weights are teaching values only.
